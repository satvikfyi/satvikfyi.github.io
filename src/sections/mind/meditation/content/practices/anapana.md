---
name: Mindfulness of Breath
slug: anapana
sanskritName: Ānāpānasati
kind: practice
summary: Attention resting on the natural breath, the first meditation the Buddha taught and the one every tradition keeps returning to.
tradition: Buddhist ānāpānasati; adopted across the yoga traditions
effects:
  - Settles attention on one simple, always-available object
  - Reveals the mind's restlessness without fighting it
  - Builds the concentration (samādhi's seed) that deeper practices need
  - A lifetime practice disguised as a beginner's one
precautions:
  - Watching the breath can make some people feel short of breath, let the breath stay uncontrolled and merely observed
  - Intense restlessness in early weeks is normal; shorter sittings beat abandoned ones
steps:
  - Sit comfortably upright; let the eyes close or lower.
  - Find the breath where it is clearest, nostrils, throat or belly, and rest attention there.
  - Know the in-breath as an in-breath; the out-breath as an out-breath. Nothing else to do.
  - When the mind wanders (it will, a thousand times), notice where it went, and gently return.
  - The return IS the practice, begin again, without commentary, for the whole sitting.
durationGuidance: Start with 10 minutes daily; grow slowly toward 20–30.
sources:
  - title: Ānāpānasati Sutta
    author: the Buddha (Majjhima Nikāya 118)
    reference: mindfulness of in-and-out breathing
  - title: The Heart of Yoga
    author: T. K. V. Desikachar
---

The Ānāpānasati Sutta is essentially this page's content, twenty-six centuries older, and blunter:
know the long breath as long, the short breath as short. Every tradition since has dressed it up; none
has improved it. The wandering-and-returning that beginners take as failure is the very muscle being
trained.
