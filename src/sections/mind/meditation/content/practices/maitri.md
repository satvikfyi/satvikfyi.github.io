---
name: Loving-Kindness Practice
slug: maitri
sanskritName: Maitrī Bhāvanā
kind: practice
summary: Deliberately wishing well, to yourself, a loved one, a stranger, a difficult person, everyone; until the wishing stops being an effort.
tradition: Buddhist mettā; maitrī in the Indian yogic texts
effects:
  - Softens chronic irritation and resentment at the root
  - The Yoga Sūtras name maitrī as one of the four attitudes that purify the mind (1.33)
  - Warms a meditation practice that has gone dry and mechanical
  - Trainable, the heart's attention strengthens like any other
precautions:
  - Beginning with oneself is hardest for many, start with someone easy if so
  - For the difficult person, choose mildly difficult first; forgiveness is gradual
  - Strong grief may surface; let it, gently, and keep the phrases simple
steps:
  - Sit comfortably; recall someone you love easily and vividly.
  - Silently repeat, feeling, not reciting; "may you be happy, may you be well, may you be safe."
  - Direct the same phrases to yourself; then to a neutral person; then to someone difficult.
  - Let the circles widen, your street, your city, all beings everywhere.
  - End by resting in whatever warmth has gathered, however faint.
durationGuidance: 10–20 minutes; excellent alongside or before breath practice.
sources:
  - title: Yoga Sūtras of Patañjali
    author: Patañjali
    reference: sūtra 1.33, friendliness toward the happy, compassion for the sorrowful
  - title: Mettā Sutta
    author: the Buddha (Sutta Nipāta 1.8)
---

Patañjali and the Buddha, a few centuries apart, prescribe the same medicine: the mind is purified by
practised friendship. The Sūtra lists it with compassion, joy and equanimity; the Mettā Sutta unfolds
it into an unbounded warmth "as a mother protects her only child". Neither promises it will be easy, 
only that it can be trained.
