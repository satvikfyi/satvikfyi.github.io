name: Mantra Meditation
slug: japa-meditation
sanskritName: Japa Dhyāna
kind: practice
summary: Repetition of a chosen name or syllable, with a mala, on the breath, or in silence; giving the mind a sound to return to instead of its own noise.
tradition: pan-Indian, vedic to tantric to everyday bhakti
effects:
  - Gives the busyness of the mind a rhythmic occupation
  - The mantra's meaning ripens slowly with years of repetition
  - A portable, eyes-open-anywhere practice
  - Traditionally held to purify and steady the heart (citta-śuddhi)
precautions:
  - Choose a mantra with meaning to you, or take one from a teacher or tradition; either is legitimate
  - Mechanical speeding to "finish the round" loses the practice's heart
  - Strong emotional release can occur with long practice; gentleness, always
steps:
  - Sit tall; decide the count, a mala's 108, or five minutes, or the length of a walk.
  - Repeat the mantra with full voice a few times, then whisper, then silently.
  - Let the repetition ride the breath; when the mind leaves, return with the next repetition.
  - In the silence after the last repetition, listen; that silence is the practice's fruit.
  - Rise slowly; let the mantra recur by itself through the day if it will.
durationGuidance: One mala (108) or 10–20 minutes, daily; tradition favours regularity over length.

    author: across the purāṇas and tantric literatures
    reference: repetition as the simplest and highest sadhanā for this age

Japa is the most democratic practice in the Indian traditions, no posture mastery, no breath control,
only a name repeated until the repeater changes. The Mantra module of this site holds the texts,
transliterations and meanings; this page is about the repeating itself.
