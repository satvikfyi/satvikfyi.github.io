We tend to picture karma yoga, the yoga of action; in professional clothing: doing the work
without clinging to the outcome, meeting after meeting. The tradition's own examples are humbler.
The Bhagavad Gītā was spoken to a warrior, yes, but the path it describes was carried for centuries
in kitchens, and it still works best there.

## Why the kitchen

Cooking contains every ingredient of the practice:

- **There is real work**, physical and skilled, that cannot be faked.
- **The fruit is immediate**, someone eats, and just as immediately out of your hands.
- **Repetition is guaranteed.** Nobody attains enlightenment by cooking dinner once.
- **The recipient is usually someone you love**, which is what makes offering, rather than
  performing, possible.

## The three conversions

An ordinary dinner becomes practice through three small interior moves, none of them visible from
outside:

1. **Cleanliness as respect.** The counter wiped before the pot is set, the vegetables washed with
   attention, the tradition treats śaucā (purity) not as hygiene anxiety but as the first gesture
   of offering.
2. **Attention as presence.** The [āyurvedic](/body/ayurveda/) ideal of food cooked in a good state
   of mind is not superstition; it is the observation that attention is the one ingredient that
   touches everything.
3. **The fruit given away.** Cook as if for a guest, serve prasāda-style (the wiki’s [worship pages](/wiki/) explain the term),
the cook eats last, and thanks whatever is worth thanking first. The Gītā's
   [half-verse](/soul/action/) about acting without claiming the fruit fits on a ladle.

## A week of it

Try one week of cooking with the [satvik planner](/body/meals/planner/): pick your dosha, shop the
list, and cook each meal as the day's central act rather than its interruption. The effects are
undramatic, which is the point; steadier hands, a quieter evening, and the strange contentment of
work that was never yours to keep.
