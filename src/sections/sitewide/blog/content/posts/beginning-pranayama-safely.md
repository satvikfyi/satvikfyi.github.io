Every yoga teacher eventually says it: the breath is more powerful than the posture. What they say
less often is that this is precisely why the haṭha texts are so careful with it. The Haṭha Yoga
Pradīpikā spends its second chapter on prāṇāyāma and begins with warnings, practice without
preparation, it says, and the breath disturbs more than it settles.

Here is the order the tradition itself suggests for a beginner, every technique named below has
its own page in the [prāṇāyāma collection](/mind/pranayama/).

## 1. Learn to breathe completely first

Before any technique, the the three-part breath: belly, ribs, chest, lying
down. Most of us breathe into the top third of the lungs and call it a day. Two weeks of ten slow
full breaths, and the rest of prāṇāyāma becomes twice as easy.

## 2. Make the two halves of the breath equal

Equal breathing, inhale and exhale the same quiet length; teaches
the steadiness everything else is built on. It is also the most portable practice on this site:
four silent counts on a bus is a legitimate sādhanā.

## 3. Add sound, then alternation

The the ocean breath gives the ear something to follow; the
the bee breath turns the volume of the mind down in ninety seconds. Then,
and only then, alternate nostril breathing; the queen of the
gentle practices, done without any breath retention for the first months.

## 4. Leave the powerful ones for later

Kapālabhāti and bhastrīka are
wonderful and demanding, the tradition is explicit that bhastrīka belongs under a teacher's eye.
They are described on this site so you can understand them; understanding is free, and attempting
them alone is not the plan.

## The one rule

The Pradīpikā's real instruction hides in plain sight: *the breath is never forced*. Dizziness,
ringing ears, anxiety; these are not "deep experiences"; they are the practice saying stop. Ten
comfortable minutes daily beat forty heroic ones every time.
