Set on the eve of a catastrophic war, the Gītā compresses the spiritual wisdom of late-Vedic India
into a battlefield conversation. Arjuna, the greatest archer of his age, throws down his bow at the
sight of his own kin across the lines; Kṛṣṇa's eighteen-chapter answer moves from duty through
devotion to metaphysics, and has been read as the mature fruit of the [Upaniṣads](/wiki/scriptures/upanishads/)
planted in the soil of action.

## What it teaches

The Gītā is the source-text for three of the paths on this site, karma yoga (act without claiming
the fruit), jñāna yoga (discern the self that never changes) and bhakti yoga (love as the final
method). Its genius is refusing to pick one: what suits the warrior suits the householder differently.

## Where to start

Chapter 2 (the sāṅkhya teaching and the famous "action without fruits" verses), then chapter 12
(the short, devotional chapter most people love first). This site's [soul paths](/soul/) carry the
key verses with translations.
