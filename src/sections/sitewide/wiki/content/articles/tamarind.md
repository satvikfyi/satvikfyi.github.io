Tamarind arrived in Indian cooking so early that its sourness structures whole
cuisines: puliyodharai in the Sri Vaishnava temples, sambar and rasam in the South,
gojju and pulikachal concentrates, and the sweet-sour chutneys of the North. The block of
pressed pulp keeps for months; a fistful soaked in hot water and squeezed gives the juice
most recipes mean by "tamarind".

The classical pairing is with [jaggery](/wiki/ingredients/jaggery/) — sour and sweet
measured against each other, as in sambar or saunth chutney. In Odia temple cooking the
sourness often comes instead from ambula (sun-dried raw mango), a reminder that the souring
agent was always regional before it was standardised.
